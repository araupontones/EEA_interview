# Findings

The most relevant findings of the analysis so far: municipal waste per person, decoupling from economic growth, the distance to the 2025 recycling targets, and the treatment of municipal waste across the waste hierarchy. Figures are for 2023 (the latest year with data for all 27 Member States) unless stated, from the Eurostat release in `data/catalog/raw_sources.csv`. How each figure is built, and its limits, is in [method_notes.md](method_notes.md).

Last updated: 15 November 2026. Update this file when the data or the method changes.

## 1. Municipal waste per person

- **The EU generates about 510 kg of municipal waste per person** (2023; 513 kg in 2024). The level has hardly changed since 2011 (499 kg): +2.2 % in 13 years. In total, about 229 million tonnes in 2023.
- **Large differences between countries.** In 2023, waste per person is highest in Austria (782 kg), Denmark (759 kg) and Luxembourg (718 kg), and lowest in Romania (305 kg), Poland (367 kg) and Estonia (373 kg). Austria generates 2.6 times as much per person as Romania. The median Member State is at 489 kg; 12 Member States are above the EU figure (510 kg). Austria's and Luxembourg's levels include waste from businesses after their change in definition, and Estonia's includes packaging waste from 2014 (see below).
- **Since 2011, waste per person fell in 13 Member States and rose in 14.** The largest falls are in the Netherlands (-18 %), Sweden (-14 %) and Denmark (-12 %).
- **The largest rises come from changes in definition, not from more waste.** Czechia (+68 %), Slovakia (+52 %), Belgium (+51 %) and Austria (+37 %) widened their definition of municipal waste (Czechia in 2017, the others in 2020) to include similar waste from businesses. Only Slovakia was already rising before the change. Comparisons over time for these countries need a note.
- **Luxembourg also widened its definition, in 2016.** Its waste per person jumped by 34 % in one year (607 to 815 kg) when it started to define municipal waste by waste codes, including more waste from sources other than households. Its net rise since 2011 is only +7.8 %, because waste per person was falling before the change.
- **Two more countries count more waste because of a change in what is included.** Estonia added packaging waste in 2014 (+22 % in one year) and Slovenia added waste from special waste collectors in 2013 (+14 %). Their rises since 2011 (+24 % and +25 %) are partly due to these changes.
- **Three large jumps have no documented cause**: Romania 2011 (+17 %), Finland 2022 (+17 %, then +10 % in 2023) and Latvia 2015 (+11 %). They should be checked with the reporters before they are read as real changes.
- **The EU figure includes the same changes.** EU waste per person rose from 505 to 521 kg in 2020 and peaked at 534 kg in 2021, partly because of the new definitions. The real rise since 2011 is probably smaller than the +2.2 % shown.
- **Per day, each person in the EU generates about 1.4 kg of municipal waste** (2023: 510 kg / 365 = 1.40 kg; 1.41 kg in 2024). Across Member States, from 0.8 kg in Romania to 2.1 kg in Austria and Denmark (2023, rounded to one decimal; Austria 2.14 kg, Denmark 2.08 kg, Romania 0.84 kg). Figures are kg per person per year divided by 365 and rounded; they are averages over a year, not a measured daily amount, and include waste from businesses similar to household waste where the national definition does (`mw_pc_day` in `municipal_waste.json`).
- **In the year of the change in definition, waste per person jumped by 14 % to 75 %.** One-year change in kg per person, from the year before the change to the year of the change: Belgium +75 % (2019 to 2020: 416 to 729 kg), Czechia +44 % (2016 to 2017: 339 to 489 kg), Austria +42 % (2019 to 2020: 588 to 834 kg), Luxembourg +34 % (2015 to 2016: 607 to 815 kg) and Slovakia +14 % (2019 to 2020: 421 to 478 kg; +13.5 % unrounded). The range, 14 % (Slovakia) to 75 % (Belgium), covers the five countries that widened their definition to include waste from businesses. The two changes in what is included are within it: Estonia +22 % (2013 to 2014: 293 to 357 kg) and Slovenia +14 % (2012 to 2013: 362 to 414 kg). Total amounts (tonnes) rose by about the same percentages, so the jumps do not come from population changes (Luxembourg +37 %, as Eurostat gives; the others within 1 point of the per-person change).
- **Ireland has a break in waste per person in 2014 and no figures for 2013 and 2015.** Eurostat: "The break in time series for 2014 (Generation) data is because of an improved methodology for estimating unmanaged household waste" (env_wasmun annex "Country specific notes on municipal waste", section "Municipal waste generated", Ireland). The annex does not explain the missing years. Irish waste per person was 585 kg in 2012 and 562 kg in 2014 (-3.9 % in two years), so the break has no large visible effect; how much of the change comes from the new method is not known. On the decoupling chart, the Irish waste line has gaps in 2013 and 2015 (GDP has figures for both years).
- **Among Member States without a change in definition or a break since 2011, Denmark generates 2.5 times as much waste per person as Romania** (2023: 759 kg / 305 kg = 2.5). The comparison leaves out the 8 Member States with a break year in waste per person since 2011 (Czechia, Belgium, Austria, Slovakia, Luxembourg, Estonia, Slovenia and Ireland; the countries with a `break_year` in `data/lookups/decoupling_notes.json`), so 19 Member States remain; the next highest are Cyprus (661 kg) and Germany (613 kg), the next lowest Poland (367 kg) and Sweden (392 kg). Across all 27 Member States, the ratio is 2.6 (Austria 782 kg / Romania 305 kg). The restricted ratio is used because Austria's level includes the waste from businesses added by its 2020 change in definition (+42 % in one year), so part of the gap with Romania comes from what Austria counts. Romania has a large jump in 2011 (+17 %) with no documented cause, but no break year, so it stays in. The dashboard computes this ratio from the data in the same way, excluding the geographies with a `break_year` in `decoupling_notes.json`.json`.
- **Comparisons between countries in the same year also need care, because what countries count as municipal waste is not fully the same.** In 2023, the levels of the five countries that widened their definition (Czechia, Belgium, Austria, Slovakia, Luxembourg) include similar waste from businesses; three of them are among the four highest in the EU (Austria 782 kg, Luxembourg 718 kg, Belgium 688 kg). Since Directive (EU) 2018/851, EU law defines municipal waste as waste from households and "mixed waste and separately collected waste from other sources, where such waste is similar in nature and composition to waste from households" (Waste Framework Directive, Art. 3(2b)), and Eurostat states that "From reference year 2020 on municipal waste generation and recyling [sic] are covered by the Directive (EU) 2018/851" (env_wasmun metadata, section 6.1). Eurostat also notes that "between 2019 and 2022 several countries adapted there [sic] data collection system to the new definition", with breaks marked `b` (section 3.4). The metadata does not say that all Member States now apply the definition in the same way; its section on geographical comparability still states: "the extent to which waste generated by offices and small businesses are included differs from municipality to municipality. Thus, different levels of municipal waste generation can reflect different coverage of the generation of waste, but also differences in the organisation of municipal waste management" (section 15.1). The data cannot settle it either: Belgium's and Slovakia's 2020 changes are not flagged as breaks, and for the other Member States a missing jump does not show whether they already counted waste from businesses. So a gap in kg per person between two countries in one year can partly reflect what each country counts, not only how much waste is generated. Sources: Directive (EU) 2018/851, https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2018/851/oj; Eurostat env_wasmun metadata, https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/cache/metadata/en/env_wasmun_esms.htm (definition in method notes, section 2).
- **Waste generated per person is the indicator used to track waste prevention, but it measures the result, not prevention itself.** Prevention means measures taken before something becomes waste that reduce its quantity (including through re-use), its impacts or its hazardous content (Waste Framework Directive, Art. 3(12)). Member States must monitor prevention with indicators, "notably on the quantity of waste that is generated" (Art. 9(3)). Eurostat: "Reducing municipal waste generation is an indication of the effectiveness of waste prevention measures" (cei_pc031 metadata); the EEA's waste prevention monitoring framework "identified municipal waste per capita as the most appropriate indicator for prevention of municipal waste" (EEA, 2025, p. 34). Prevented waste itself cannot be counted. The link holds for the change over time: differences between countries in one year mainly reflect consumption and what each country counts as municipal waste (EEA, 2025, p. 27; bullet 13), so the levels are not a ranking of prevention effort. Sources (checked by the fact-checker, 15 November 2026): Directive 2008/98/EC as amended, consolidated text https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2008/98/article/3 and /article/9; Eurostat https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/cache/metadata/en/cei_pc031_esmsip2.htm; EEA, "The feasibility of additional measures to strengthen reuse and waste prevention" (2025), https://www.eea.europa.eu/en/circularity/knowledge/technical-reports/the-feasibility-of-additional-measures-to-strengthen-reuse-and-waste-prevention.

## 2. Decoupling from economic growth

Waste per person compared with real GDP per person, both indexed to 2011 = 100.

- **In the EU, waste per person is decoupled from economic growth in relative terms.** From 2011 to 2023, real GDP per person rose by 15.6 % and waste per person by 2.2 % (2024: +16.8 % and +2.8 %). Because of the breaks in series, decoupling in the EU is probably stronger than the index shows.
- **13 Member States show absolute decoupling**: the economy grew while waste per person fell. The clearest cases are Ireland (GDP index 197, waste 96), Malta (157 and 97), Bulgaria (146 and 97), and the Netherlands (113 and 82), which has the largest fall in waste.
- **9 show relative decoupling**: waste per person grew, but more slowly than the economy. Examples: Lithuania (GDP index 159, waste 101), Romania (156 and 118), Poland (149 and 115).
- **5 show no decoupling: Czechia, Belgium, Austria, Slovakia and Luxembourg.** For four of them the result comes from a change in definition: before the break, Belgium and Luxembourg showed absolute decoupling, and Czechia and Austria relative decoupling. **Slovakia is the only Member State where the lack of decoupling is real**: its waste per person rose faster than GDP before its 2020 change (+35 % against +22 % from 2011 to 2019).
- **Irish GDP per person overstates the growth of the Irish economy, so Ireland's decoupling looks stronger than it is.** In our data, Ireland's real GDP per person rose by 23.5 % in 2015 alone (EU27: +2.2 %) and by 97.1 % from 2011 to 2023 (EU27: +15.6 %). The Irish Central Statistics Office (CSO) explains the 2015 jump by multinational companies moving their balance sheets, mostly intellectual property, to Ireland: "The level shift in GDP of 26% in the 2015 results was driven by relocations of entire balance sheets to Ireland." (CSO's 26 % is total real GDP in its 2016 release; our 23.5 % is per person, in 2020 prices, from the current Eurostat release.) The Central Bank of Ireland states that "rankings based on per capita GDP data place Ireland much too high" because of "the statistical distortions created by the activities of multinational corporations". The fall in Irish waste per person (index 96 in 2023) is not affected; only the size of the GDP rise is overstated. Sources: CSO, https://www.cso.ie/en/methods/nationalaccounts/din/nie2017faq/; Central Bank of Ireland, https://www.centralbank.ie/news/article/press-release-economic-letter-is-ireland-really-the-most-prosperous-country-in-europe-04-january-2021.
- **In the year before each change in definition, waste per person was decoupled from GDP in all countries except Slovakia.** Indices (2011 = 100) in the year before the break: Belgium 2019, waste 91.4, GDP 106.9 (absolute decoupling: waste fell while GDP grew); Luxembourg 2015, waste 91.1, GDP 100.4 (absolute, but GDP per person had grown by only 0.4 % since 2011); Estonia 2013, waste 97.3, GDP 106.3 (absolute); Czechia 2016, waste 105.9, GDP 109.1 (relative: waste grew more slowly than GDP); Austria 2019, waste 102.6, GDP 105.4 (relative); Slovakia 2019, waste 135.4, GDP 121.9 (no decoupling: +35 % against +22 %). Slovenia does not fit any class before its 2013 change: in 2012 both fell (waste 87.2, GDP 96.9), so "waste fell while GDP grew" does not apply to it.
- **Decoupling waste from economic growth is the stated aim of the EU's waste prevention programmes.** Waste Framework Directive, Art. 29: "The aim of such objectives and measures shall be to break the link between economic growth and the environmental impacts associated with the generation of waste." So the decoupling chart shows the prevention step over time. Source (checked by the fact-checker, 15 November 2026): https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2008/98/article/29.

## 3. Distance to the 2025 recycling targets

Recycling rates in 2023, two years before the 2025 deadline, compared with the 2025 targets. Rates are calculated under the 2025-target methods. A negative distance means the country is below the target.

### Municipal waste (target: 55 %)

- **The EU recycles 47.9 % of its municipal waste, 7.1 points below the 2025 target.**
- **Only 6 Member States reach 55 %:** Germany (68.7 %), Austria (62.8 %), Slovenia (59.8 %), the Netherlands (57.9 %), Luxembourg (56.5 %) and Belgium (55.8 %).
- 3 more are within 5 points: Italy (50.8 %), Latvia (50.6 %) and Slovakia (50.3 %).
- **6 Member States recycle less than 30 %:** Romania (12.4 %), Cyprus (15.8 %), Bulgaria (16.7 %), Greece and Malta (17.4 %), and Poland (27.6 %). Poland's 2023 rate is not comparable with its earlier rates (40.9 % in 2022): from 2023 its treatment data are reported directly by companies instead of being partly estimated, and Eurostat flags recycling in 2023 as a break in series (method notes, section 2).
- **Postponements.** 8 Member States postponed the 2025 municipal target and must reach at least 50 % by 2025. In 2023, only Slovakia (50.3 %) was above 50 %; Lithuania was close (49.3 %). The other six (Greece, Croatia, Hungary, Malta, Poland, Romania) were below 37 %. 13 Member States that did not postpone were below 55 %, including Bulgaria and Cyprus, which are below 20 %.

### Packaging (target for all packaging: 65 %)

24 Member States have rates for 2023. Bulgaria, Czechia and Hungary have none under the 2025-target method.

- **The EU recycles 67.4 % of its packaging waste, 2.4 points above the 2025 target.** 11 of 24 Member States reach it, and 8 more are within 5 points.
- **Plastic is the main gap.** The EU recycles 41.6 % of plastic packaging, 8.4 points below the 50 % target. Only 5 of 24 Member States reach it: Belgium, Germany, Latvia, Slovenia and Slovakia. Denmark (22.7 %), France (25.7 %) and Austria (26.9 %) are more than 20 points below.
- **The other materials are mostly on track.** Paper and cardboard: 19 of 24 Member States reach the target (EU 87.0 %). Steel: 17 of 23 (EU 84.9 %). Wood: 17 of 24 (EU 37.7 %). Aluminium: 16 of 24 (EU 56.0 %). Glass: 13 of 24 (EU 74.9 %), with 6 more within 5 points.
- **Leaders and laggards.** Belgium, Germany, Slovenia and Slovakia reach all seven packaging targets. Belgium, Germany and Slovenia also reach the municipal target: they are the only Member States on track for all eight targets. Romania and Malta miss all seven packaging targets, and Greece misses six.
- Some postponements apply. 7 Member States (Czechia, Greece, Croatia, Luxembourg, Malta, Portugal, Sweden) postponed some material-specific targets, but not the target for all packaging. Which materials each postponed is not known yet, so their misses on materials cannot yet be read as failures.

## 4. Treatment of municipal waste across the waste hierarchy

What happens to the municipal waste generated, as a share of waste generated (not of waste treated, so the shares are measured like the recycling rate and the targets). Steps of the hierarchy: preparing for re-use; recycling (material recycling, and composting and digestion); other recovery (incineration with energy recovery); disposal (landfill, and incineration without energy recovery). Prevention has no share: prevented waste is not generated. Source: `municipal_waste_treatment.json`; mapping and decisions in method notes, section 2 ("Treatment across the waste hierarchy").

- **In the EU, almost half of municipal waste is recycled, a quarter is burned for energy, and a fifth is landfilled** (2023): recycling 47.7 % (material 28.9 %, composting and digestion 18.8 %), incineration with energy recovery 25.3 %, landfill 21.5 %, incineration without energy recovery 0.5 %. The remaining 5.0 % of waste generated is in no reported operation (see the gap below). The EU landfill and incineration-without-recovery figures for 2023 are our estimate, as Eurostat publishes none (Latvia missing); the effect on the shares is under 0.1 point.
- **Recycling in the hierarchy (47.7 %) and the target measure (47.9 %) differ only by preparing for re-use.** Both are shares of waste generated in 2023, from the same Eurostat data. The 0.2-point gap (478 thousand tonnes) is the preparing for re-use that some countries report inside recycling (mostly France, Belgium, Austria, Denmark, Latvia and Ireland). By country the gap is up to 2.5 points (Latvia), 1.4 (Belgium) and 0.7 (France). The EEA defines the municipal recycling rate the same way as the hierarchy, "material recycling and composting/anaerobic digestion", and reports 47.7 % for the EU in 2023 (EEA indicator "Waste recycling in Europe", 3 December 2025, https://www.eea.europa.eu/en/analysis/indicators/waste-recycling-in-europe). The EEA used an older Eurostat download (Ireland 2020 and Latvia 2022 data in place of 2023), so the match with our figure may be partly coincidental. For the targets, the EEA counts preparing for re-use and recycling together (EEA briefing 28/2022). Because the hierarchy shows no EU figure for preparing for re-use, the small amount prepared for re-use is inside the 5.0 % "not in the figures".
- **Preparing for re-use is very small, and there is no EU figure.** Eurostat publishes no EU total, and 8 Member States do not report it (Germany, Estonia, Spain, Croatia, Italy, the Netherlands, Poland, Portugal). The 19 that do prepared 476 thousand tonnes for re-use in 2023, about 0.2 % of EU waste generated; the highest shares are Latvia (2.5 %) and Belgium (1.4 %). For the hero ladder: the step exists but is too small to show as a share of the EU total.
- **Since 2011, the EU has moved its waste up the hierarchy.** From 2011 to 2023, landfill fell from 33.5 % to 21.5 % of waste generated (-12.0 points), and incineration without energy recovery from 5.3 % to 0.5 %. Recycling rose from 38.9 % to 47.7 % (+8.8 points) and incineration with energy recovery from 19.8 % to 25.3 % (+5.5 points). In 2024: landfill 20.8 %, recycling 49.2 %, energy recovery 25.9 %. The change is gradual, with no sudden jumps in the EU series.
- **Landfill still dominates in 7 Member States.** More than half of municipal waste generated is landfilled in Greece (80.7 %), Romania (75.7 %), Malta (73.9 %), Cyprus (61.5 %), Portugal (56.6 %), Hungary (54.2 %) and Croatia (51.8 %). 15 Member States landfill more than 10 % (Latvia has no 2023 figure), the maximum for 2035 under the Landfill Directive (measured with the target's own rules, which can differ slightly from these shares).
- **9 Member States landfill less than 5 %**: Austria, Belgium, Germany, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Luxembourg, the Netherlands and Sweden. Most of them burn much of the rest for energy: in Sweden (58.6 %), Finland (54.0 %) and Denmark (51.0 %), more than half of municipal waste is incinerated with energy recovery. In 5 Member States, more waste goes to energy recovery than to recycling: Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Ireland and Sweden.
- **Composting and digestion are a large part of recycling.** EU-wide they are 18.8 % of waste generated, against 28.9 % for material recycling. In the Netherlands (30.2 % against 27.7 %) and Portugal (17.5 % against 13.0 %), more waste is composted or digested than recycled as material.
- **In 7 Member States, more than a tenth of the waste generated is not in the treatment figures** (2023): Bulgaria (35.9 %), Poland (21.5 %), Cyprus (20.4 %), Estonia (18.9 %), Slovenia (17.5 %), Italy (14.1 %) and Croatia (10.6 %). In Portugal, more waste is treated than generated (-5.4 %). Eurostat lists possible reasons: estimates for people not covered by waste collection, weight losses, exports and imports, temporary storage, waste counted in two treatment steps, and, from 2020, recycling measured after sorting losses (env_wasmun metadata, section 15.4). The data does not show which applies to each country. For these countries, the shares add up to less (or more) than 100 %, and the ladder should show the remainder as "not accounted for" rather than spread it over the steps.
- **Poland's 2023 figures contain a break caused by a change in method.** Waste generated stayed the same (13.4 million tonnes), but waste treated fell by 2.9 million tonnes and recycling from 5.5 to 3.7 million tonnes: Poland's recycling rate fell from 40.9 % in 2022 to 27.6 % in 2023, and 21.5 % of its waste is in no operation, against 0 % in every earlier year. Eurostat documents the cause: "Until 2022, municipal waste treatment data was partially estimated. From 2023, the data on municipal waste management processes are reported in the M-09 directly by companies, excluding the estimate." The change "caused a drop in municipal waste recycling by 41% in 2023" and "a difference between waste generation and treatment of 20%" (env_wasmun annex "Country specific notes on municipal waste", Poland). Eurostat flags 2023 as a break (`b`) for waste treated, recycling and landfill; not as estimated or provisional; waste generated is not flagged. So the fall shows a change in measurement, not in waste management, and 2023 cannot be compared with earlier years. It is most of the rise in the EU share not accounted for (from 2.9 % in 2022 to 5.0 % in 2023). Bulgaria's treatment figures are also unstable from year to year since 2020.

## 5. Caveats for all findings

- **Breaks in series** affect municipal waste per person and decoupling for Czechia, Belgium, Austria, Slovakia, Luxembourg, Estonia and Slovenia, and the EU figure. The other flagged breaks have small effects (under 10 %). Jumps in Romania, Finland and Latvia have no documented cause (method notes, section 6).
- **The municipal recycling rate is Eurostat's official indicator (cei_wm011).** The EU 2023 value (47.9 %) and the country values match Eurostat's "Recycling rate of municipal waste" (cei_wm011, data updated 15 October 2026); France 2023 is flagged as estimated. It is recycling (RCY) as a share of waste generated, and includes composting and digestion. Since Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2019/1004, Eurostat counts preparing for re-use as recycling, but in the data only some countries include it (method notes, section 2). Eurostat's first release gave 48.0 % for 2023 (February 2025), revised since. Neither this rate nor the hierarchy share is the compliance figure reported under Decision 2019/1004. Sources (checked by the fact-checker, 15 November 2026): https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/api/dissemination/statistics/1.0/data/cei_wm011 ; https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/cache/metadata/en/cei_wm011_esmsip2.htm (section 4.1); https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/de/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20250213-1.
- **Some packaging rates are above 100 %** (Slovenia, Poland) and overstate the distance to target. The Cyprus steel rate (221.5 %) is left out of the assessment (method notes, section 3).
- **Treatment shares do not always add up to 100 %**, because treatment is not always equal to generation; the gap is above 10 % in 7 Member States and 5.0 % for the EU in 2023. **Poland's 2023 treatment and recycling figures contain a break** (Eurostat flag `b`), caused by a change in method (treatment reported directly by companies instead of partly estimated), which also affects its recycling rate and distance to the municipal target (27.6 % in 2023, against 40.9 % in 2022) (method notes, section 2).
- **2023 is a snapshot, not the 2025 result.** Countries below the target in 2023 still had two years to reach it, and postponements change what counts as a failure (method notes, section 4).
